Cross-Channel Ad Spend Halo — Laura Geller (US)

Does spend on one channel cause orders credited to a different channel? 104 weeks, US-only, all weeks included (no holiday exclusion), permutation-tested with false-discovery-rate control (q ≤ 0.10 = named).

Headline: 2 of 12 channels show a real, statistically defensible halo — Meta and AppLovin. Nothing else clears the bar. The earlier version of this page reported 12.4% of orders misattributed across 22 named channel pairs — that number came from an estimator with two bugs (a holiday-exclusion choice that was propping up significance instead of fixing the actual problem, and pooling of the US and UK markets, which turned out to be statistically invalid) and is not supportable. This page reflects the corrected model.

Why there's no per-pair table

The original ask for this page was per-pair numbers — "Meta spend causes X orders credited to Pinterest" — so credit could be moved channel-by-channel. Once the estimator bugs above were fixed, the corrected model showed that split isn't statistically identifiable with this data: once a channel's overall halo is estimated, there's no reliable residual heterogeneity left across destinations to say which channel absorbed it. A channel's halo is a property of the channel, not of any specific channel pair. Naming individual pairs (as the old page did) fits noise, not signal.

What the data does support: Meta and AppLovin both create incremental demand (confirmed by refitting on total new orders, not just relabeled credit) somewhere outside their own channel. Practically, that means Meta's and AppLovin's true CAC/ROAS are better than their last-click numbers show, and whichever channels are getting that overflow credit look correspondingly worse than their true CAC/ROAS — but which specific channels, and by how much, isn't answerable from this data source alone.

A caveat worth flagging

Both named channels are holiday-dependent: refitting with November/December excluded shifts Meta's z from 2.85 to 2.13 and AppLovin's from 2.29 to 3.08. Both survive either way, but the sensitivity means the estimate isn't fully stable across the calendar — worth another look once more data outside the holiday window accumulates.

Model: calc2.py, US market only, 104 weeks, one parameter per lever, moving-block bootstrap standard errors, permutation credibility on a standardised statistic, Storey q-values, incremental-demand refit per lever on total new orders.
